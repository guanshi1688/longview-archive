# Core Terms｜The Economics of Productive Organisation

These are the terms as used in this manual of economic operation. For the historical formation of organisational forms, consult the common terms and canonical texts of *Civilizational Structural Algorithm*. The definitions here place those concepts in observable economic relationships; they do not establish another system of civilizational classification.

## Productive Capacity

Real productive capability created through people, technology, equipment, energy, facilities, knowledge, and cooperation. It is not synonymous with output or capital stock. Its continued significance depends on whether it can be used, maintained, and renewed.

## Production Organisation

Arrangements of responsibility and cooperation that coordinate the conditions of production and keep them in existence. They may operate within firms and across households, regions, public agencies, and international networks. “Organisation” must not be reduced to a public administration or an internal corporate chart.

## Production-Bearing Organisation

An organisational form principally responsible for continuously forming, maintaining, and reproducing productive capacity. It may use prices, companies, contracts, and finance; it does not exclude interfaces.

## Interface-Oriented Organisation

An organisational form principally responsible for connecting, accessing, settling, and reallocating capabilities among actors, including productive capacity outside its direct productive scope. It can also build factories and carry public productive obligations; it does not mean “does not produce.”

## Interface

A mechanism through which the capabilities, rights, needs, and payments of different actors can be identified, connected, and, where appropriate, brought to an end. Examples include prices, contracts, property rights, standards, credit, procurement, platforms, and settlement. An interface is a practical capacity for organising production, not production's opposite.

## Callable Productive Capacity

Capacity that a specified actor can actually access when needed, at a usable time, quality, cost, legal status, and level of risk. The existence of production somewhere in the world does not guarantee stable access to it.

## Internal Reproduction Pressure

The pressure on an organisation to preserve the conditions for the next production round when it cannot reliably obtain capability externally or continually transfer the consequences of failure elsewhere. It concerns facilities, reserves, skill formation, and continuing responsibility. Its strength depends on concrete exit options and access to external capability, not on a supposed psychological trait of a nation.

## Internal Pressure on People

The requirements for sustained effort, time commitments, learning, cooperation, and responsibility when internal reproduction pressure reaches workers, households, and particular organisers. It may be transmitted through institutions, education, professional norms, and cultural expression. It does not mean that overwork is good, nor does an abstract assertion that a people are “hard-working” replace empirical study of human capability formation.

## Human Reproduction and Renewal

Processes and conditions allowing people to live, recover, raise the next generation, learn, and re-enter productive activity after work changes. It is part of capability continuity, alongside equipment renewal, but people are not machine components that may be assigned arbitrarily.

## Self-Reproducing Production Centre

An organisational network that can continuously train people, renew facilities, retain suppliers, fix faults, secure orders, and invest again under normally accessible input and market conditions. It need not make every component locally or be entirely self-sufficient.

## Productive Responsibility

Identifiable obligations for forming, operating, maintaining, absorbing the failure of, and renewing a productive or supply capability. Asset ownership, profits, and responsibilities may belong to different actors.

## Residual Productive Responsibility

Responsibility for the essential supply, recovery, and capability maintenance left uncovered by ordinary markets and contracts. It is not a synonym for permanent state operation of all production.

## Interface Power

The capacity to influence others' production and allocation of orders through customer relationships, channels, standards, finance, procurement, or settlement terms. Its specific services, choices, and constraints must be identified.

## System Contribution

The effect of a capability upon other productive activity, continuity, speed of recovery, or future viable choices. It cannot simply be equated with the capability's market price, total social welfare, or the income to which an actor is entitled.

## Effective Demand

Demand accompanied by the ability and willingness to pay under specified prices and conditions. It is not identical to every unmet need or to orders won by a particular firm.

## Sustainable Orders

Repeat purchases whose volume, payment, quality requirements, and timing are sufficient to sustain the associated capability's maintenance, reasonable costs, and next round of operation. A single large order may be useful without proving commercial independence.

## Productive Surplus

Capacity exceeding orders actually obtainable or effective demand under a specified market and organisational structure. It is not a claim that every human material need has been fulfilled.

## Social Absorption

How households, enterprises, social organisations, and public institutions share the costs of life and productive failure so that people can continue living, learning, working, consuming, and re-entering production. It is neither an unlimited guarantee nor only a fiscal transfer.

## Bounded Responsibility

Responsibility that can be legally recognised, delimited, and terminated when stated conditions are met. Actors may still carry contractual, familial, and public duties. It does not mean that individuals have no social obligations.

## Localised Exit

Termination of a particular project, actor, or relationship while the larger productive network and essential capabilities continue. It requires alternatives, settlement rules, routes for people to transfer, and appropriate support.

## Systemic Reformatting

A high-loss historical outcome in which cascading failures across responsibility accounts and productive nodes force a broad rebuilding of productive conditions and old organisational relations. It is a risk to avoid, not a policy instrument for correction.

## Organisational Feedback

The way an organisational form, through sustained success, failure, and accumulated capability, changes the next round's markets, costs, risks, feasible solutions, and distribution of responsibility. The original mechanism may remain useful or meet new limits.

## The Production–Consumption–Reproduction Circuit

The connected process through which productive capacity forms, orders are obtained and fulfilled, output is used, returns and risks are distributed, human life is supported, and capabilities are renewed so that production and access can occur again. The circuit may cross borders; it does not mean national self-sufficiency.
