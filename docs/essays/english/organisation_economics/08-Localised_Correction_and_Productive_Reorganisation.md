# 08｜How to Correct Errors Without Forcing an Entire Production System to Reset

## 08-01｜Organisational Success Creates Organisational Inertia

Building capacity over many years creates machinery, institutions, personnel, public finance, and debt relationships. A mature organisation knows how to repeat a task but does not necessarily know when to stop repeating it. A locality skilled at attracting factories may keep launching projects after its next constraint has shifted from lack of capacity to lack of orders.

Interface organisations also have inertia. A persistent preference for short-term returns and rapidly reversible investment may impose expensive rebuilding costs when essential skills and facilities need long-term preservation.

What is needed is therefore neither simply “more state” nor “more market.” Identify who notices each error first, who bears it, and how adjustment can occur without destroying indispensable capability.

## 08-02｜Why Localised Exit Matters

Changes in prices, losses, contract expiry, bankruptcy, and the movement of customers may reveal that an old relationship no longer fits reality. When losses can be accounted for and assets and people have routes back into productive activity, localised exit frees resources for the next organisational arrangement.

A garment factory losing a long-standing customer, for example, may retool or find another buyer without closing an entire region's industry. But if local government revenue, business debt, and household income can survive only if that factory keeps expanding, a small operating problem can become a shared regional burden.

**Letting an error end locally is one way to protect the larger production system.**

## 08-03｜Why Liquidation Alone Is Not Enough

After a railway is liquidated, factories along the route may have no comparable transport. Once a skilled workforce in basic materials disperses, the next batch of orders may not bring those people back. Local financial settlement must therefore occur alongside identification of system functions.

Do not begin by asking whether to “save the firm” or “save the market.” First separate assets that no longer have a productive use, workers who can transfer, facilities that must continue, actors who absorb temporary gaps, and the authority to carry out reorganisation.

Separating the function from its present owner helps avoid two deadlocks: retaining every old debt to preserve a capability, or destroying every capability to remove the debts.

## 08-04｜Interfaces Can Serve as Corrective Devices

Market prices, customer choices, financing conditions, competition among localities, and social feedback can reveal changes that administrators may not observe promptly. A system with continuing public productive responsibilities can still use open procurement, operating-performance measures, competing service providers, and explicit exit rules.

An interface supplies information, however, not automatically correct information. Monopoly, subsidies, external risks, insufficient information, and different time horizons can influence prices. Financial returns may diverge from lasting productive capability through leverage or short-term expectations. No market outcome should simply be declared an efficient selection.

Ask what an interface measures, what it leaves out, who bears the effects, and whether the interface itself can be corrected.

## 08-05｜Shaping Conditions Versus Prescribing Results

To develop a productive capability, an organiser may directly specify companies, quantities, and routes. Alternatively, it may improve education, infrastructure, competition, quality standards, long-term orders, basic research, and the conditions for re-entry after failure.

The methods are not mutually exclusive. Emergency repair requires clear direction. The long-term development of complex industries requires countless firms to experiment in uncertain conditions. An important boundary is that the harder it is to know future winners, the more reason there is to invest public resources in conditions that allow several viable answers to compete, rather than declare one irreversible plan.

## 08-06｜Four Levels of Correction

```text
Business:  Is there actual demand for the product?
Actor:     Must the original company survive?
Capacity:  Which machines, skills, and services should remain?
System:    Where will exit costs land in households, the region,
           and the next production round?
```

The four levels need not all receive the answer “retain” or all receive “exit.” A product may cease, a firm reorganise, skills transfer, and a public capability pass to a new operator.

## 08-07｜Why Debt Restructuring Cannot Replace Industrial Reorganisation

Extending a company's debts may temporarily ease cash-flow pressure. It may not change product demand, equipment competitiveness, the availability of engineering talent, or future orders. These are physical and market questions.

Conversely, a company with real orders and technology may be unable to pay suppliers or employees because of its old debt structure. Insisting on immediate shutdown can then destroy sustainable capability. Restructuring the accounts may buy time for productive reorganisation. The two forms of restructuring may support one another, but neither is a substitute for the other.

The same holds for localities. New financing instruments that extend repayment on old projects do not automatically create new social income. Cancelling all old projects does not prove that households and businesses have acquired alternative livelihoods. Financial maturities, actual supply, and household income must be reconnected.

## 08-08｜One Possible Reorganisation Process

Suppose a region's established manufacturing industry loses competitiveness as products change. The first step is not to declare all existing assets obsolete. Inventory the facilities, workers, and suppliers that could serve new products. Next distinguish activities to continue, convert, retrain for, and close. Third, ensure households and public services can sustain the transition. Fourth, use customer needs, purchasing commitments, test orders, and cost checks to assess whether the new activity can continue independently.

Some jobs may disappear and old companies exit; skills and transport infrastructure may remain useful. Counting only newly launched projects conceals whether old capability transferred successfully. Counting closures alone equally fails to show whether the organisation renewed itself.

## 08-09｜Management Must Learn, Not Merely Execute

Departments good at meeting specified engineering targets may be less good at learning about future products from dispersed customer feedback. Actors skilled at financial exit may be reluctant to carry long-term obligations for public capacity that cannot quickly be priced.

Changing organisation does not mean declaring either skill mistaken. It means allocating information and responsibility to fit different tasks. Emergency repairs need command and closed responsibility. Technological exploration needs trials and competition. Long-lived infrastructure needs maintenance budgets. Occupational transitions need support for daily life. Using one target and one accountability system for all of them can generate rigid execution and unowned obligations together.

## 08-10｜Who Has an Incentive to Report Bad News?

Every organisation needs genuine feedback. If a project sponsor is rewarded only for the number of new builds, maintenance costs may be understated. If a manager is rewarded only for meeting profit targets, training and equipment renewal may be deferred. If a public service is judged only by complaints, the hardest people to serve may be excluded from the count.

Correction needs more than metrics: it must examine the behaviour metrics induce. Different actors need ways to report orders that cannot be fulfilled, recurring faults, overdue payments, and household risks before a system-wide failure forces recognition.

Statistics should be cross-checked against conditions on site. No single indicator simultaneously measures technical capability, orders, and social reproduction. The more an indicator becomes the only goal, the more an organisation may optimise the appearance of success instead of the underlying function.

## 08-11｜Make Project Exit and Capability Retention Practicable

When a long-running project ends, at least four inventories matter: transferable assets; contracts to fulfil or renegotiate; people's retraining and relocation options; and regional functions that cannot stop. Different responsible parties should check them. “The project has closed” does not settle every consequence.

On this view, governing capability includes not only concentrating resources to complete a project but openly identifying error, allocating losses reasonably, retaining reusable capability, and ending obligations that no longer fit.

## 08-12｜Reorganisation Ultimately Reorganises Responsibility

Changing a company's name, increasing a project budget, relocating a factory, or paying a subsidy does not necessarily change the old circuit. Ask whether responsibility for building capacity, obtaining orders, household risks, failure settlement, and maintenance costs has been realigned.

**Genuine correction does not make the system incapable of failure. It gives failure boundaries, essential capability a means of continuation, and resources a route into the next round of effective production.**
